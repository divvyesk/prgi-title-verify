0:00 – 1:30 DIVVYE Integration lead · contracts, FastAPI orchestrator, semantic scoring & fusion ~210 words
Slide 1 on screen. Stand centre. Do not start until the room is quiet.

Good morning. We’re Team 404 Found, and we’re here with PSS06 — automated press title admissibility clearance for the Press Registrar General of India.

Here is the situation today. Under the Press and Registration of Periodicals Act, 2023, no periodical in India can publish until its title has been cleared against every title already on the register. That register holds 82,713 entries. The check is manual. It takes twenty-five to thirty days. And if you are rejected, you are not told why — you pay roughly a thousand rupees again and start over.

Advance to slide 2.

Two completely different things can sink a title, and this is the insight the entire system is built on. One — it is confusingly similar to something already registered. Two — it breaks a statutory naming rule, regardless of similarity. Those are structurally different problems. The obvious move is one classifier for both. We deliberately built two separate mechanisms — a four-dimensional similarity engine, and a deterministic rule engine — because only one of them is allowed to guess.

I own the contracts and the FastAPI orchestrator that ties all of this into a single verify call. Pruthviraj will start with the half that is not allowed to guess.

1:30 – 3:00 PRUTHVIRAJ Rules corpus · deterministic rule engine · evaluation data ~215 words

I own the rules — which means I own whether you can trust anything else on this screen.

The PRGI Guidelines for Admissibility of Titles is a real document. I went through it one sentence at a time — every “shall not”, every “is not admissible” — and turned each one into a single machine-checkable rule with the clause attached verbatim. Not a paraphrase. The actual sentence. That is  __  rules extracted,  __  implemented and tested.

Two things I want to be explicit about, because they are the difference between a demo and a product.

First — where I could not find the clause, the rule is flagged as unverified and appears in our coverage report as a gap. We publish what the system cannot check. An invented clause number would discredit every other number we show you.

Second — the rule engine is plain Python. No language model decides admissibility. Same title, same rule, same answer, every time. That is what auditability means when a rejection is a legal act.

There is exactly one rule that needs a model — catching a banned meaning hidden inside a translated title. That one returns an advisory flag and requires human confirmation. It can never auto-fail a title.

Jai owns the half that is allowed to be fuzzy.

3:00 – 4:40 JAI Lexical, phonetic, core-word & BM25 matching · composite scorer ~255 words · demo runs inside this block
Gurpreet drives. Have the four demo titles pre-typed in a scratch note, not typed live.

Similarity is where the manual process actually fails, because a title does not have to be identical to be confusing. We score every candidate on four independent dimensions.

Lexical — RapidFuzz, and specifically token-sort ratio, which sorts the words before it compares them. That is what catches “Times India” against the registered “India Times”. [DEMO 1 — Times India]

Phonetic — Double Metaphone. But Double Metaphone was designed for English surnames and it is weak on transliterated Indian names, so we added a second signal: the consonant skeleton. Strip the vowels. Jagran becomes J-G-R-N. Jaagran becomes J-G-R-N. Same code. Indian romanisation varies almost entirely in the vowels, so that one cheap rule catches most of the real phonetic collisions Metaphone misses. [DEMO 2 — Jaagran]

Semantic — multilingual embeddings over pgvector. “Dainik Samachar” and “Daily News” mean the same thing in two languages and land in the same vector neighbourhood. Nothing about the characters is similar. [DEMO 3 — Dainik Samachar]

Core-root — strip the media filler words: The, Daily, Weekly, Express, News. “The Vidarbha Daily Express” reduces to Vidarbha, and collides with the registered Vidarbha Patrika. [DEMO 4 — The Vidarbha Daily Express]

Those four are merged with Reciprocal Rank Fusion, which combines ranked lists by position rather than score — because a trigram score and a cosine similarity are not on the same scale and cannot be averaged.

And one rule overrides the blend: a perfect clash on any single dimension is disqualifying on its own. A title that sounds exactly like an existing one is a conflict even if it is spelt completely differently. Suhani.

4:40 – 6:10 SUHANI Agentic Title Studio · RAG explainer · presentation ~225 words

Catching a bad title is half a product. Telling the applicant what to do next is the other half. Two pieces.

The explanation. When a rule fires, we do not ask a model “why was this rejected”. We retrieve the actual guideline passage from the database first — vector search over the chunked guideline text — and only then ask the model to phrase that retrieved text in two plain sentences. Retrieve, then generate.

And there is a guard. If the model’s output cites a clause that was not in what we retrieved, we throw the output away and quote the clause verbatim instead. That is how we can say we did not hallucinate, rather than hope.

The Agentic Title Studio. Four agents in a LangGraph graph — Interviewer, Generator, Verifier, Ranker. The Interviewer takes the publication’s genre, state and language and writes a brief. The Generator proposes fifteen to twenty candidates. The Verifier sends all of them back through the same five-stage pipeline you just watched — not a lighter check, the real one. And if fewer than five survive, it loops back to the Generator with the rejection reasons attached, up to three times.

So every alternative we put in front of an applicant has already passed the exact test that failed their original. We never show an unverified suggestion. Gurpreet.

DIVVYE — architecture, backend, integration, performance
Is this actually working, or is it a mockup?
Working. What you just saw came over HTTP from a FastAPI service querying PostgreSQL — the badge on the result says which engine answered, and I can show you the network tab. The offline engine is a genuine fallback against a smaller sample, not a fake. If any pipeline stage is still on its stub flag on the day, name that stage explicitly and say what is real. Do not round up.
Six people, three days. How did you not step on each other?
Contracts frozen at noon on day one — every request and response shape defined once, in Pydantic and in Zod, plus seven hand-written fixture files. After that nobody waited for anybody: Suhani’s agents ran against a fixture client, Darsh’s docket against fixture cases. Swapping a fixture for a real HTTP call is one line. On top of that, strict folder ownership — no two people edit the same directory.
How do you actually hit under two seconds?
Four things. The embedding model loads once at startup, not per request — that alone is the difference between thirty seconds and thirty milliseconds. The four retrievers run concurrently. The two hundred shortlisted candidates are fetched in one query, not two hundred. And there is an in-memory cache on the normalised title.
Why merge with Reciprocal Rank Fusion instead of averaging the scores?
Because a trigram similarity and a cosine similarity are not on the same scale, so averaging them is meaningless. RRF only looks at position — first place in any list is worth one over sixty-one, tenth is worth one over seventy. A title near the top of two different lists beats one that is first in only one, and it needs no calibration between scorers that were never comparable.
Why PostgreSQL and not a real vector database?
82,713 vectors is small — Pinecone and Milvus are built for hundreds of millions. Adding one would mean a second service, a second network hop per query, and keeping relational metadata and vectors in sync across two systems, for no gain at this size. With pgvector, one query filters on state and language and ranks by vector distance at the same time. If the register grows by two orders of magnitude that gets revisited — it is documented as a future migration, not an oversight.

PRUTHVIRAJ — rules, citations, evaluation data, script coverage
Show me the guideline clause behind that rejection. Where exactly does it come from?
[Open the rule and read the clause field aloud.] That sentence is copied verbatim from section  __  of the Guidelines for Admissibility of Titles. Every rule row carries the sentence and the section it came from, and the rule code reads its citation out of that file at import time — so the code and the citation physically cannot drift apart. And here is the coverage report: every restriction we found in the document, and whether we implemented it.
What happens when you could not find the clause?
The rule is flagged unverified and shows up as a gap in the coverage report. We publish what the system cannot check. A missing rule is a documented limitation; an invented clause number would discredit every citation in the system, so we never write one.
Your keyword lists will over-fire. Won’t “News” flag half the register as commercial?
That is exactly the failure we tested for. A single common word never triggers a rule on its own — we require a phrase or a second signal. We ran the whole rule set against fifty known-good registered titles and counted the false positives.
Your dataset is entirely Latin script. How can you claim multi-script support?
Correct, and we say so on the slide. The 82,713 real records are Latin-script only — that is a property of the source data, not a design choice. To demonstrate the cross-script path we generated a small native-script set with the indic-transliteration library, flagged in the database as synthetic. It is a demonstration set, back-transliteration is lossy, and it is labelled that way everywhere. The pipeline handles the script; we simply do not have real native-script registry data yet.
What is your accuracy?
We report shortlist recall at two hundred —  __ %  on a  __ -pair evaluation set built from real near-duplicates in the register, the six benchmark scenarios, and hand-made reorderings, misspellings and translations. We deliberately do not quote a precision figure, because there is no ground-truth set of historical PRGI rejections to measure against. That is listed as an open risk, and it is exactly why a human stays in the loop on amber cases.
JAI — matching algorithms, scoring, tuning
Why four dimensions? Isn’t one good model enough?
Because they fail in different places. Lexical cannot see that Dainik Samachar means Daily News. Semantic cannot see that Jaagran sounds like Jagran. Core-root catches Vidarbha buried inside The Vidarbha Daily Express, which nothing else does cleanly. Each is cheap; together they cover the failure modes the manual process actually misses.
Double Metaphone is English-centric. Doesn’t it break on Indian names?
It does — which is why we added the consonant skeleton alongside it. Strip the vowels and compare what is left: Jagran, Jaagran and Jagaran all reduce to J-G-R-N. Indian romanisation varies almost entirely in the vowels, so that one rule catches most of what Metaphone misses. We take the higher of the two signals.
Where do the weights come from? Aren’t they arbitrary?
They live in a config file loaded at startup, never hardcoded, because the spec requires them tunable. They were tuned against the evaluation set — we tried several combinations and kept the one with the best recall, and the committed file records the number it scored.
Scoring two hundred candidates on four dimensions — isn’t that slow?
Under three hundred milliseconds for all four. Nothing loops in Python over candidates — the lexical scorer runs vectorised in C++ against all two hundred at once, and the semantic scorer encodes the whole batch in a single call.
What is BM25 doing that trigram search isn’t?
Trigram matching treats every character sequence as equally informative. BM25 weights by rarity — matching “Vidarbha”, which appears in about forty of 82,713 titles, counts enormously more than matching “News”, which appears in thousands. That distinction is precisely what this problem needs.

SUHANI — agents, RAG, explanation integrity
How do you know the explanation isn’t hallucinated?
Two things. Order — we retrieve the guideline passage before the model is called, and the prompt instructs it to use only that text. And a guard — we check that the output cites a clause that was actually in what we retrieved. If it does not, we discard the output and quote the clause verbatim. So the worst case is a blunter explanation, never a fabricated one.
Why LangGraph rather than CrewAI or AutoGen?
Because this is not an open-ended agent conversation — it is a fixed four-step sequence with exactly one bounded retry. LangGraph expresses that as explicit nodes and edges with inspectable state at every step. The autonomous frameworks are built for looser collaboration, which here makes behaviour harder to constrain and audit — a disadvantage when the output has to be traceable.
Are those generated titles actually verified, or just generated?
Actually verified, through the same five-stage pipeline, in one batch call — not a lighter check. If a generated title fails, it never reaches the applicant. That is why the Verifier is a node inside the graph rather than a step after it.
What if the loop never finds five good titles? Or the API dies mid-demo?
The loop is bounded at three attempts, then returns whatever survived with an honest count — there is no unbounded loop anywhere in the graph. For the API: there is a deterministic template generator built on day one, and responses are cached on disk keyed by prompt hash, so a repeated run is instant and needs no network.
Isn’t suggesting titles overstepping? That’s the applicant’s creative decision.
It is optional, and it is a starting point rather than a submission — the applicant edits them or ignores them entirely. The value is that the ones we show have already cleared the check that failed their original, so they are not walking into a second rejection blind.

DARSH — officer workflow, audit trail, accessibility, testing
Does this replace the PRGI officer?
No — and the interface is deliberately built so it cannot read that way. The system produces a recommendation with evidence; the officer takes one of two explicit actions. Even the memo is editable and requires an officer action before it counts. The District Magistrate’s endorsement step is untouched; it is out of scope by design.
Why does the officer see the same screen as the applicant?
So they cannot be looking at different facts. We reuse the same components rather than building a second display, which means no version of the evidence exists on only one side. If the applicant is told their title clashes with India Times at ninety-four percent, that is the exact number in the officer’s docket.
What is the audit trail on a decision?
Each action produces a recorded artifact — a reference token and a timestamp appended to the case record. The exportable memorandum then contains the full basis for that decision: the title, the verdict, all four sub-scores, the clash table, every rule violated with its verbatim clause, and the plain-language summary.
Have you actually tested this, or does it only work in your demo?
 __  end-to-end browser tests that drive a real browser — type the title, click verify, assert the verdict and the clash table — one per benchmark scenario. Plus one that runs with the backend deliberately stopped, so the fallback is covered by a test rather than by hope.
Is it usable by a colourblind officer?
Every verdict colour is paired with an explicit text label — approved, manual review, rejected — never colour alone. The verdict region announces itself to a screen reader when it arrives, the docket is fully keyboard-navigable, and we specifically checked the amber-on-warm-background contrast, which is the combination that usually fails.
